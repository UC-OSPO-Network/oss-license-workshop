## Glossary

Definitions marked TBD are still to be written. See [issue #26](https://github.com/UC-OSPO-Network/oss-license-workshop/issues/26).

Permissive license
: A license, such as BSD or MIT, that allows you to use, modify, and redistribute the code with little restriction. It leaves no proprietary control to the original owner.

Copyleft license
: A license that requires any modified or unmodified code you redistribute to be licensed under the same form of license. The University of California (UC) calls these licenses "hereditary". They are sometimes called "viral".

Hereditary
: UC's term for a copyleft license. See copyleft license.

Patent-granting license
: A license that grants users a license to the copyrights in the code and also gives all later users rights to any patent of the licensor that the code needs, including patents the licensor gets in the future. GPL 3.0 and Apache 2.0 are examples.

Delegated authority
: At UC, the campus office that can approve open source licensing of work the Regents own. It is usually the campus technology licensing, copyright, or patent office. UC's guidance on the risk of each license is advice only. The delegated authority approves every case.

Dual licensing
: Releasing the same software under two different licenses. It needs the agreement of all authors and, where campus rules require it, the delegated authority.

Copyright
: Applies to original works of authorship. It protects the expression of an idea, but not the idea itself. It applies automatically and does not require a © notice. The copyright owner has the exclusive right to copy, distribute, modify, perform, and display the work.

Patent
: Applies to inventions. An invention must meet five criteria: patentable subject matter, utility, novelty, non-obviousness, and enablement. A patent is not automatic: the inventor must apply for one. A patent gives the right to use, make, sell, offer for sale, and import.

License
: Permission from the copyright or patent owner to use, copy, distribute, make, or sell the work or invention. The owner can set restrictions or obligations on use in the license.

Open source license
: A license that is royalty-free and permits the licensee to use, copy, modify, and distribute the software. The source code is made available to end users so they can exercise those rights.

Royalty-free
: End users do not pay any money.

Licensee
: The end user, who is permitted to use, copy, modify, and distribute the software.

Strong copyleft
: A copyleft license at the far end of the spectrum from permissive licenses. GPL 2.0 is an example: it requires your code to be placed under the terms of the GPL.

Weak copyleft
: A copyleft license that typically limits either the scope of what counts as a derivative work, or the trigger for the copyleft provision. For example, the LGPL may allow you to dynamically link to an LGPL library without triggering the copyleft provision.

Tech Transfer office (TTO)
: The campus office you check with before you release code under an open source license. It is sometimes called the Office of Intellectual Property or Technology Commercialization. It verifies ownership and funding requirements, makes sure no third-party restrictions apply, and helps you select a license from the UC-recommended list. In UC guidance this office is usually the delegated authority. See delegated authority.

The Regents of the University of California
: At UC, the typical owner of software created using university resources, or in connection with a research project or a staff position.
