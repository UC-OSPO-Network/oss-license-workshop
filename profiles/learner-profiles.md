---
title: Learner Profiles
---

These profiles describe the people this lesson is for. They come from the target audience notes the authors wrote during lesson design. The names are fictional.

Each profile answers the four questions the Carpentries lesson development training asks about a learner: what is their background, what can they already do, what do they want to do with the skills, and what problem does the lesson help them solve.

## Camila, a researcher with software ready to share

Camila is a postdoctoral researcher. She wrote scripts and a small tool to analyze her lab's data, and other labs have asked to use them. She knows how to write and run code. She has heard that she needs a license, but she does not know the difference between the options, and the number of choices feels confusing.

She wants to share the software and pick a license that fits her goals. She also needs to know when to ask her institution before she decides, for example when the university owns the code.

This lesson helps her choose and apply a license with confidence. She is the main audience, because she matches the people who contact a university open source program office (OSPO) with a project already in hand.

## Mateo, a curious newcomer

Mateo works in a campus research group and uses open source tools every day. He has never thought about the license behind them. A colleague mentioned that "no license" can cause problems, and he wants to understand why.

He does not have a project to release yet. He wants a clear picture of what an open source license is, what the main types allow, and what to watch for when he reuses other people's code.

This lesson gives him a foundation for later decisions about using and sharing open source software.

## Elias, a learner who wants to know why

Elias supports research computing for a department. People ask him which license to use. He can follow a flowchart, but he wants to understand the reasons behind the different choices, so that his advice holds up in new situations.

This lesson gives him the reasoning behind permissive and copyleft licenses, including the cases where they conflict.

The design notes list this audience as a secondary one. The lesson does not build for it in the first version.

## Valentina, a researcher who can share only part of a project

Valentina leads a small research software project. Some of the code is ready to share. She wants to keep some of it closed for now. She also installed packages through a package manager along the way and has never looked at their licenses.

She wants to know which parts can go out under an open license, and whether the licenses on her packages allow it. A license that looks safe for one part might clash with a package used in another.

This lesson shows her how license choices combine, and what to check before she releases a project that mixes open and closed parts.

## Santiago, a staff member who wrote code the university owns

Santiago works as research staff and wrote a tool for a campus group as part of his job. A collaborator at another institution asked to use it. Santiago assumes the choice of license is his to make.

Under University of California policy, code written for a staff position is typically owned by The Regents of the University of California, so the decision does not rest with him alone. He needs to find out who approves a release, and then pick a license from the list the university recommends.

This lesson walks him through the ownership question first and the license choice second. It ends with Santiago adding the license to the repository.

## What all five share

- They need no knowledge of licensing before the lesson.
- They know, at a basic level, what open source software is.
- They want to make informed decisions, not just follow a rule.
